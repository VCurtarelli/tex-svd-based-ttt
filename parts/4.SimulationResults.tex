To compare the two presented techniques, a simulated environment was created, and both techniques were employed to estimate the SSF. Namely, we will compare the proposed method as obtained in \cref{eq:sec3:minim_solution_proposed} (named SVD), with the literature standard technique from \cref{eq:sec2:minim_solution_literature_normalized} (named TTT). In this section, we present the environment and practical conditions assumed, detail the considerations taken for the exhibited techniques, as well as other underlying necessary parameters, methods, and metrics; and finally, we bring forth the results and their subsequent discussions.

\subsection{Environment conditions}
\label{subsec:sec4:environemnt_conditions}

\input{figures/4.discretized_ssf_with_profiles.tex}
    
The sound speed field was obtained by combining the temperature, depth, and salinity data from the World Ocean Atlas (WOA) \cite{garcia_world_2024} with the \texttt{GSW} Python package \cite{mcdougall_getting_2011}, which implements the TEOS-10 model. A total of $N_{\x} = 9$ temperature and salinity (yearly) profiles in a location near $-36.5\dg$S, $-50.5\dg$W (south of Brazil) were selected and extracted from the WOA database; and their respective velocity fields were generated using the \texttt{GSW} package. This produced a consistent, but not uniform, set of nine profiles. Given the database's limitations, the environment was discretized vertically into $N_{\z} = 29$ cells (downsampled by 2 from WOA data), totaling $N = 9\cdot 29 = 261$ cells; also, the maximum depth considered was $z = 1500\si{\meter}$. The considered SSF is presented in \cref{fig:sec4:discretized_ssf_with_profiles:subfig1}, with its profiles --- each 1D slice for a fixed $\x$-axis offset --- being shown in \cref{fig:sec4:discretized_ssf_with_profiles:subfig2}. By convention, the $\z$-axis always points downwards.

We also consider two device distributions (both with $9$ sources and $9$ receivers), presented in \cref{fig:sec4:discretized_ssf_with_profiles:subfig3,fig:sec4:discretized_ssf_with_profiles:subfig4}: in the first case (the surround scenario), the sources are positioned at the bottom and on the right-side, with receivers positioned near the surface and on the left-side; and in the second case (the layered scenario), the sources are strictly aligned at the bottom and the receivers are close to the surface only. In both cases and figures, the sources are represented by blue circles, and the receivers by red Xs. We assume that all devices are at the center of their respective cells for simplicity.

The ``surround''' scenario more closely aligns with conditions commonly described in the literature \cite{ali_opensource_2019,holstein_acoustic_2004,hormati_robust_2010,jeong_investigating_2024}, especially considering that this technique is most commonly used for biomedical applications. 
%, where ocean bottom nodes \cite{cantillo_ocean_2010} --- commonly used as receivers for oceanic subsurface imaging --- are repurposed as sources for water-column environment modeling, and remotely operated vehicles traversing in the surface are used as receivers.
Meanwhile, the ``layered'' scenario imposes more restrict constraints on the devices, and implies that the waves of interest are predominantly travel vertically.

For the forward problem (estimating $\bvR{i}$ given $\bvs{i}$), the \texttt{EikonalFM} Python package \cite{ganster_eikonalfm_2025} will be used, implementing the Fast Marching Method \cite{tang_travel_2024} for solving the first arrival-times along the grid points. This aligns with the standard approach described in the literature.

\subsection{Metrics and evaluation}

To reflect the objectives of TTT, two error metrics will be used. The first metric reflects the primary optimization objective of minimizing the travel-time error, and is given by the root mean squared error (RMSE) based on the cost function given in  \cref{eq:sec2:def_optimization_and_cost-function},
\begin{equation}
	\rmse_t(\bvs{\est}) = \Sqrt{\frac{1}{M} E(\bvs{\est})},
\end{equation}
with $\bvs{\est}$ being the estimated slowness field (in the final iteration). We will also use the RMSE of the sound speed field between the true ($\bvc{\true}$) and estimated ($\bvc{\est}$) SSFs,
\begin{equation}
	\rmse_{\ssf}(\bvs{\est}) = \frac{1}{\Sqrt{N}} \norm{\bvc{\true} - \bvc{\est}},
\end{equation}
where $\bvc{\est} = \frac{1}{\bvs{\est}}$ (element-wise reciprocal). $\rmse_t(\bvs{\est})$ reflects the explicit objective of travel-time error minimization, while $\rmse_{\ssf}(\bvs{\est})$ represents the implicit underlying objective of estimating the original SSF  (\cref{fig:sec4:discretized_ssf_with_profiles:subfig1}).

\subsection{Regularization matrix}

As discussed, the selection of a regularization matrix will control which metric will be adjointed to the primary objective of travel-time error minimization. We chose a discrete Laplacian approach, improving smoothness in the outcome. We assume that $\bvs$ is constructed by row-vectorizing \cite{curtarelli_generalized_2023} a $\bvS \in \re^{\sz{N_{\z}}{N_{\x}}}$ matrix, where each entry corresponds to its cell's slowness. More precisely, the relationship between the elements of $\bvs$ and $\bvS$ is $\el{\bv{S}}[j,k] = \el{\bvs}[N_{\x}j+k]$, with $0 \leq j < N_{\z}$ and $0 \leq k < N_{\x}$. With this, $\bvD$ can be written in block form, with $N_{\z}$ blocks ($\bvQ$, $\bvQ'$, $\Id{N_{\x}}$, $\bv0~\in\re^{\sz{N_{\x}}{N_{\x}}}$) in each direction,
\begin{equation}
	\bvD = \begin{bmatrix}
		\bvQ' & \Id{N_{\x}} & \bv0  & \cdots & \bv0\\
		\Id{N_{\x}} & \bvQ & \Id{N_{\x}} & \cdots & \bv0\\
		\bv0 & \Id{N_{\x}} & \bvQ & \cdots & \bv0\\
		\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
		\bv0 & \bv0 & \bv0 & \cdots & \bvQ'
	\end{bmatrix},
    \label{eq:sec4:construction_bvD}
\end{equation}
\begin{subequations}
	\begin{gather}
		\bvQ' = \begin{bmatrix}
			-2 & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
			1 & -3 & 1 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
			0 & 1 & -3 & \cdots & 0 & 0 \\
			\vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots & \vdots \\
			0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & -3 & 1 \\
			0 & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 1  & -2
		\end{bmatrix}, \label{eq:sec4:construction_bvQ_bvQ':subeq1}\\[2mm]
		\bvQ = \bvQ' - \Id{N_{\x}}.
	\end{gather}
\end{subequations}

\subsection{Estimation parameters}

For the TTT, we will use the $\alpha$ (normalized) regularizing parameter. While the SVD derivations present the controlling parameter as $\tilde{R}$, we will use the parameter $\beta$, truncating any singular value $\lambda_n$ such that $\lambda_n < \beta \lambda_1$ (with $\lambda_1$ being the largest singular value). For the ``surround'' scenario, the TTT technique will use $\alpha = 3.5133$, and the SVD will use $\beta = 0.2490$. For the layered scenario, the TTT will use $\alpha = 7.0179$, and the SVD will use $\beta = 0.6735$. These were chosen through a hyperparameter search, \textsl{a posteriori} selecting the ones that minimize the SSF error. This way we can compare the optimal performance of each technique, in each scenario.

Regarding the iterative minimization (alternating forward and inverse maps), we will limit it to $10$ iterations, as we empirically noticed no further improvements above this value.

\subsection{Metric results}

The $\rmse_{t}$ and $\rmse_{\ssf}$ metrics over 10 iterations, for both techniques (TTT and SVD) and scenarios (surround and layered), are shown in \cref{fig:sec4:tt_ssf_rms_errors}. Both plots' $\y$-axis are in a log-scale.

In the surround scenario, both methods exhibit nearly identical performance. The difference in $\rmse_{t}$ between the two remains negligible throughout the iterations, and both approaches converge to an $\rmse_{\ssf}$ of approximately $1.2\si{\meter/\second}$.

In contrast, for the layered scenario, clear differences are found. While the SVD presents constant $\rmse_{t}$ and $\rmse_{\ssf}$ behaviors over the iterations, the traditional TTT's errors increase as the minimization process progresses. This indicates a mismatch between the forward map (through the FMM) and the inverse map (through the TTT), leading to a non-functional minimization process. While the SVD's errors remain constant and mitigate this mismatch, both are indicative of overfitting. Nevertheless, in the layered scenario both methods perform significantly worse than their surround scenario counterparts, with SSF errors more than one order of magnitude larger (at the final iteration).

This behavior can be attributed to the limited spatial diversity of ray paths in the layered scenario. The rays propagate predominantly vertically, with limited angular variation, resulting in weak coupling between measurements. Consequently, the inversion becomes underconstrained, leading to solutions that fit the travel-time data, but poorly represent the true SSF. In contrast, the surround configuration provides rays with a wider range of directions, increasing interdependence among measurements and improving reconstruction fidelity.

\subsection{Estimated sound speed profiles}

\Cref{fig:sec4:estimated_ssfs} shows the difference between the estimated SSPs (for each $\x$-axis offset) and the reference (from \cref{fig:sec4:discretized_ssf_with_profiles:subfig2}) for both methods and scenarios, with each marker corresponding to a cell. We highlight the difference in the $\x$-axis range of both figures.

In the surround scenario (\cref{fig:sec4:estimated_ssfs:subfig1}), both methods closely match the true profiles, with their errors behaving almost randomly over depth; however, the trends are shared between the two methods. The error is also concentrated around $z = 1\si{\kilo\meter}$, where the true profile (\cref{fig:sec4:discretized_ssf_with_profiles:subfig2}) varies rapidly; and near the ground ($z = 1.5\si{\kilo\meter}$), where the problem is less constrained by the discrete Laplacian.

Meanwhile, both methods fail to recover the correct profiles in the layered scenario (\cref{fig:sec4:estimated_ssfs:subfig2}). Nevertheless, the SVD solution's error is smaller when compared to the error given by the TTT approach. These outcomes are consistent with the results expressed by the metrics, and confirm that insufficient spatial information leads to poor SSF reconstruction. This also suggests that modified acquisition frameworks or additional constraints are necessary for practical deployment in this scenario.
