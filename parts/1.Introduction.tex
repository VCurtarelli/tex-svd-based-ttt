\section{Introduction}

In the field of oceanic underground imaging, a faithful estimation of the water column's sound speed is important \cite{jensen_computational_2011,medwin_fundamentals_1999}. Although the speed of sound in water is considered constant at around $1500 \si{\meter}/\si{\second}$ \cite{delgrosso_new_1974}, this approximation is invalid for large-scale environments \cite{pinson_sound_2010}. In such environments, such as the water column in shallow and deep water, the speed of sound is affected by a range of variables such as pressure, temperature, salinity (abbreviated TPS), and water currents \cite{medwin_fundamentals_1999,delgrosso_new_1974}. As these properties vary over the body of water, so does the velocity, creating a sound speed profile (SSP) in a 1D scenario \cite{li_acoustic_2019,pinson_sound_2010}, or a sound speed field (SSF) in a multi-dimensional case. The SSF acronym will also be (indistinguishably) for both ``sound speed field'' and ``sound slowness field'', where slowness is the inverse of speed, given that both have a direct inverse relationship.

Knowing a given SSF enables the estimation of the behavior of a wave as it travels through this medium \cite{li_acoustic_2019,pinson_sound_2010}. By Fermat's principle of least time and Snell's law \cite{perrine_fermats_1961}, a change in speed of a wave along its trajectory also changes its direction. This causes refractions, reflections, and formation/dispersion of beams \cite{cornuelle_ocean_2008}. For a sufficiently large body of water, these wavepath transformations are noticeable and need to be accounted for. By treating an omnidirectional wave as a cluster of unidirectional waves, we can calculate the travel-time between the source and any point within the medium, as well as the wavefront's profile. The knowledge of the path taken by an ensemble of rays is of great importance in the fields of seismology \cite{bormann_seismic_2012} and oceanography \cite{cornuelle_ocean_2008}, and the knowledge of the SSF within the environment is relevant for acoustics \cite{holstein_acoustic_2004}, medicine \cite{hormati_robust_2010}, and others.

Initial works on the topic of estimating the sound speed field in water aimed to estimate the TPS, as well as the relationship between them and the sound speed. Works such as \cite{medwin_fundamentals_1999,mcdougall_getting_2011,delgrosso_new_1974} operated under this framework, with some still being used today to model the behavior of water currents on a global scale.

While early methods used the environmental TPS to estimate the SSF, most modern SSF estimation approaches avoid estimating these properties, and treat this SSF estimation as an inversion problem. That is, the knowledge of some information regarding the wave propagation within the environment enables the estimation of the SSF in the medium. Different techniques approach this inversion problem by measuring different wave properties, and using distinct mathematical models. Such techniques encompass Travel-Time Tomography \cite{stefanov_travel_2019}, Matched Field Processing \cite{gemba_adaptive_2017} and Matched Field Inversion \cite{dosso_bayesian_2011}, Full Waveform Inversion \cite{virieux_overview_2009}, a mix of the former \cite{treister_full_2017}, or a fusion with machine learning \cite{zhang_bayesian_2021}.

Among these, Travel-Time Tomography (TTT) is known for its simplicity and versatility \cite{stefanov_travel_2019,aki_determination_1977,phillips_traveltime_1991,tang_travel_2024}. It primarily uses the difference in arrival time of the acoustic signals at the receivers, these being spatially distributed in the environment (either within, or along its border). In general, the SSF is discretized in cells (or layers in the 1D scenario), wherein the sound speed is considered constant. The SSF estimation iterates alternately between calculating the paths and lengths in each cell by solving the Eikonal equation \cite{au_geometrical_2005} (generally through the Fast Marching Method \cite{tang_travel_2024}) (called the forward map); and estimating the speed in each cell (called the inverse map), which is tackled by the TTT. As previously cited, TTT is known for its simplicity, versatility, and low computational complexity. However, it depends on the precise positioning and syncing of the devices.

Many modifications of the TTT approach have been proposed, aiming to improve the quality of the inverse map estimation. Additions such as regularization \cite{aki_determination_1977}, blurring \cite{ali_opensource_2019}, and conjugate gradient minimization \cite{tang_travel_2024} (among others \cite{jeong_investigating_2024,hormati_robust_2010}) have been proposed and studied. Each of these aims to adapt the inverse problem by correcting different issues that may arise, such as ill-posedness, physics-defiant models, or incoherent solutions.

In this paper, we propose a new approach, employing the Singular-Value Decomposition (SVD) and the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse for the primary estimation problem, as well as a null-space exploiting step for achieving a minimum on a secondary (regularizing) objective function, with minimal impact on the SSF estimation. We present the necessary algebraic framework and developments, and discuss the theoretical differences between the proposed SVD method and the traditional TTT, highlighting differences in computational complexity, and scenarios where the proposed method can be computationally optimized.

Through simulations emulating real scenarios, results show that both frameworks, the traditional TTT approach and the proposed SVD approach, fare similarly in a scenario closer to one found in the literature (regarding the devices' positioning). Meanwhile, in a device distribution more aligned with  practical constraints, the SVD method shows a noticeably better performance in terms of SSF estimation, although it is underperforming in simple scenarios described in the literature.
